IGNIS · architecture explained

IGNIS runs like a well-run hospital ward for machines

This page explains why the IGNIS architecture has the shape it has. It uses one analogy throughout, a hospital, because almost every structural choice in IGNIS has a direct, familiar counterpart in how a careful hospital looks after patients. Each decision below states what was chosen, the hospital equivalent, why it was chosen, what else was considered, and what it costs.

Proposed design · nothing here is built or trained · all numbers are fictional fixtures

Companion to the animated high-level architecture. The source of record is docs/ARCHITECTURE.md, where the 14 October section describes the same design in specification form. The user confirmed this proposed-system direction on 14 October 2026. Read the complete workflow, comparison and acceptance criteria for the implementation boundaries.

The short version

If you read nothing else, read these five sentences.

  1. Machines are patients. Sensors are the bedside monitors that record all the time, and the hourly check is the nurse's ward round that looks at the last hour as a whole.
  2. The alarm comes from the numbers, not from the AI. A temperature that is rising too fast raises an alert the same way a vital-sign threshold does. No language model has to be awake for that to happen.
  3. The AI is a well-trained resident doctor, not the attending. It reads the patient's chart and the relevant guidelines, then writes a draft note in which every claim points to a source. A senior expert decides what is true and what work is authorised.
  4. Fine-tuning is the residency programme. It happens away from the ward, on reviewed past cases. It ends with an exam against the untrained baseline, and a committee signs off before any new version is allowed onto the ward. It teaches skills, not facts about today's patient.
  5. People approve every action that touches the real world: the diagnosis scope, who goes to the machine, whether the repair worked, and what gets added to the knowledge base.

Three loops that run at three different speeds

The most important structural choice is to split the system into three loops instead of one long pipeline. They run at very different speeds and need very different safeguards, just as ward rounds, treatment and residency training do in a hospital.

Monitor · every hour

Ward rounds

Speed: once per completed hour, automatically
  • Sources≈Bedside monitors
  • Ingest & review≈Admissions desk and medical coder
  • Trusted data≈The patient chart
  • Hourly check≈The nurse's round
  • Evidence & explanation≈Resident's cited note, checked by a pharmacist
  • Reports≈Signed discharge or shift report
Respond · per incident

The treatment team

Speed: minutes to hours, only when something is wrong
  • Incident & expert≈Attending physician
  • Match & dispatch≈Theatre and surgeon scheduling
  • Repair & verify≈Surgery and post-op observation
  • Learn & publish≈Case conference and the hospital handbook
Improve · offline

The residency programme

Speed: a deliberate cycle, weeks apart, never automatic
  • Fine-tuning & model release≈Training, board exam and credentialing
Curate dataset›Fine-tune (LoRA)›Evaluate›Approve & register

Coursework from reviewed cases, practice, a board exam against the untrained baseline, then a credentialing committee decision.

Why separate them? If the loops were merged into one pipeline, the slowest and riskiest step would set the pace and the risk for everything else. Training a model takes hours and needs careful evaluation, so it cannot sit inside an hourly check. Dispatching a technician needs a human decision, so it cannot be automatic like the hourly check. Keeping the loops separate lets each one have exactly the safeguards it needs. The loops connect only through a few well-defined hand-offs: a raised alert, a published case, and a released model version.

One incident, start to finish

The demo's pump-bearing scenario, told twice: once as IGNIS would run it and once as a hospital would handle the equivalent patient. Every number is a fictional fixture, and the times after 10:00 are illustrative.

09:00–10:00all hour
IGNIS

Bearing temperature, vibration and motor current readings stream in. Each one is tied to Pump P-101, its sensor and its unit. New work-order text gets extraction suggestions that wait for supervisor approval. Nothing is diagnosed per sample.

Hospital

The bedside monitor records the patient's temperature every few seconds. Nobody calls a doctor for each reading. The nurse will look at the whole hour on her round.

10:00hourly boundary
IGNIS

The hourly check assesses exactly [09:00, 10:00). Hourly mean bearing temperature has gone 72 → 76 → 80 °C, rising +4 °C per hour. If that trend continued, it would cross the fictional 85 °C limit at about 11:15. That is a conditional trend line, not a failure time.

Hospital

On her round the nurse sees the fever has risen one degree an hour for three hours. "If this keeps going, it passes 40 °C before lunch" is a trend, not a prognosis.

10:00+ seconds
IGNIS

A numeric alert is raised from the numbers alone, before any language model runs, and an incident is opened with one owner.

Hospital

The vital-sign threshold triggers a page to the doctor on call. The alarm does not wait for anyone to write a note.

after 10:00explaining
IGNIS

The knowledge graph resolves which bearing this sensor measures and which manual applies to this pump model. RAG retrieves only those passages and similar reviewed cases. Everything is frozen into an evidence pack. The reasoning adapter returns "loose mounting" and "bearing wear" as hypotheses, each citing evidence IDs, plus a suggested inspection. The claim checker rejects any number, asset or time it cannot find in the pack.

Hospital

The resident checks the wristband, pulls this patient's chart and the guideline for this condition, and writes a note: "possible infection (see lab result 14) or drug reaction (see chart entry 9); suggest blood culture." The pharmacist checks every dose and lab value against the chart before the note goes up.

~10:20expert review
IGNIS

The maintenance expert reads the checked assessment, confirms that inspection is warranted and sets the authorised task scope: inspect and re-torque the bearing mounting, with replacement allowed if wear is found.

Hospital

The attending reviews the note, agrees with part of it and authorises a specific procedure, not "do whatever seems right".

~10:30dispatch
IGNIS

Two jobs are open: J1 (this pump, $1,200/h production loss) and J2 ($300/h). Technician A lacks the qualification, so A is excluded before any cost is computed. B is free in 10 minutes, C immediately. Sending C to J1 and B to J2 costs $1,250; the reverse costs $1,400. The dispatcher approves the $1,250 plan and both technicians accept.

Hospital

The theatre scheduler won't book a surgeon without the right credential, however available they are. Then the surgeon who is free right now goes to the more urgent patient, and the other one waits ten minutes for the less urgent case.

laterrepair & verify
IGNIS

Technician C re-torques the mounting and records the work. The incident stays open until the following hourly assessments show the temperature back within the baseline. If it does not recover, the incident reopens and goes back to the expert.

Hospital

After surgery the patient stays under observation. "The operation went well" does not close the case; normal vital signs over the next hours do.

days laterlearn & publish
IGNIS

The closed case is reviewed, corrected and published. It is retrievable by RAG at once, so the next similar alert can cite it. It also joins the pool of candidates for the next fine-tuning dataset, but publication itself starts no training.

Hospital

The case is presented at the case conference and added to the ward handbook the same week. It may become teaching material when next year's residency curriculum is written.

weeks laterimprove
IGNIS

Once enough new reviewed cases exist, a training cycle runs: freeze a dataset version, train the adapters, compare them with the untuned base model on held-out cases, and the model owner approves the release or rejects it. The registry keeps the previous version as a rollback target.

Hospital

Residents finish a rotation, sit the board exam, and the credentialing committee grants privileges, which it can revoke.

The dispatch arithmetic, in full

The two plans differ only in who waits 10 minutes, and 10 minutes of waiting costs very different amounts on the two jobs:

10 min of waiting on J1 = $1,200/h × 1/6 h = $200 10 min of waiting on J2 = $300/h × 1/6 h = $50 Plan 1: B → J1 (waits 10 min), C → J2 → $1,400 Plan 2: C → J1, B → J2 (waits 10 min) → $1,250 ← $150 cheaper, the difference is $200 − $50

With two jobs and two eligible technicians there are only two plans, so checking both exhaustively gives the true optimum within this fixture. It says nothing about a whole plant.

The design decisions, one by one

Each decision opens to show the hospital analogy, the reasoning, the alternatives I considered and why I rejected them, and the cost of the choice. Some of this comes from your stated requirements: the hourly assessment in decision 2, RAG plus knowledge graph in decision 5, expert review before knowledge grows in decision 12, and the fact that fine-tuning plays a role at all. How those are realised, and every other decision, are my proposals for you to accept or change.

1

Split the system into three loops

Monitor, respond and improve run separately and connect through a few defined hand-offs.

›
Hospital: ward rounds, the treatment team and the residency programme are run by different people on different schedules, even though they share the same patients and records.

Why

  • The loops run at very different speeds: an hour, a few hours, and weeks.
  • They carry different risks. Monitoring is low-risk and automatic, responding touches real machines, and model change can affect every future decision.
  • Each can fail without stopping the others. A training cycle that fails evaluation does not stop the hourly check.

Alternatives considered

One linear pipelineEasier to draw, but it forces the slowest, riskiest step to run at the pace of the fastest and makes failures cascade.
Continuous online learningFolds the improve loop into the monitor loop. Rejected because it removes the evaluation and approval step (see decision 8).

Cost: three hand-off contracts (the alert, the published case and the model release) must be designed carefully. That is less work than untangling one giant pipeline.

2

Collect continuously, assess once per completed hour

A confirmed requirement. At 10:00, assess exactly [09:00, 10:00).

›
Hospital: the monitor records every few seconds, but the nurse reviews the patient on a fixed round. Reviewing on a schedule makes each review comparable with the last one.

Why

  • A fixed, non-overlapping window gives each assessment one clear identity, and the same data always produces the same result.
  • It prevents alert spam: a rolling window re-evaluated every minute would raise the same alert sixty times an hour.
  • It bounds the cost of explanation. The language model runs at most once per alerting hour, not once per reading.
  • Historical replay and simulated live data can share one engine, because both are processed hour by hour.

Alternatives considered

Per-sample streaming detectionFastest reaction, but noisy, hard to audit and expensive if every spike asks a model for an opinion.
Rolling one-hour window, re-evaluated every minuteExplicitly ruled out by the user: the requirement is one assessment per completed hour.

Cost: an event at 10:01 can wait until the 11:00 assessment. A separate fast critical-alarm path for hard limits was proposed as an optional add-on. It is off by default, and it never means automatic shutdown.

3

Raise the alert from the numbers, before any language model runs

Detection is deterministic code; the model only explains afterwards.

›
Hospital: the monitor alarm goes off when the vital signs cross a threshold. It does not wait for the resident to finish thinking about it.

Why

  • Reliability: if the model is slow, wrong or offline, the alert still fires.
  • Auditability: a threshold and a deviation calculation can be checked by hand, but a model's judgement cannot.
  • Reproducibility: the same hour of data always gives the same alert.
  • Language models are not good at arithmetic over thousands of readings, and code is.

Alternatives considered

LLM reads raw sensor data and decidesNon-deterministic, expensive, prone to inventing numbers, and a single point of failure.
Learned anomaly detector onlyPossible later, but it needs validated failure data, which the project does not have. It can arrive as an optional, separately approved numeric update (the dashed arrow).

Cost: fixed rules can miss subtle patterns. That is acceptable for a first system, and the release path allows reviewed rule and baseline updates.

4

Gate intake, and keep one trusted data store

Nothing reaches the assessment until it is validated, tied to an asset and, for text, reviewed.

›
Hospital: the admissions desk checks identity and puts a wristband on the patient. A medical coder turns doctors' free text into standard codes, and a supervisor audits the coding. Only then does it go into the chart.

Why

  • The most damaging errors are identity errors, such as a reading attributed to the wrong pump or a value in the wrong unit. They are cheap to catch at the door and very expensive to catch later.
  • A spreadsheet's format says nothing about what its columns mean, so meaning has to be confirmed when the file arrives.
  • One trusted store means every later conclusion can be traced to exact record versions.

Alternatives considered

Let each component read raw sourcesEach component would interpret the data slightly differently, and no single answer to "what did the system know?" would exist.
Fully automatic extractionFaster, but extraction mistakes would flow silently into the knowledge base. The extraction adapter suggests and a person approves.

Cost: supervisor time for reviewing text. The extraction adapter exists to make that review faster, not to remove it.

5

Use the knowledge graph first, then RAG (together called "KAG" here)

A confirmed requirement. The graph says what applies, and RAG retrieves only that.

›
Hospital: before opening the library, the resident checks the wristband and the chart. This patient, this condition, this drug allergy, so this guideline applies. Then she reads only the relevant pages, not every page that mentions a fever.

Why

  • Plain RAG retrieves text that sounds similar. Two pump models can have near-identical manuals with different limits, and plain RAG cannot tell which one applies.
  • The graph records structure: this sensor measures this bearing, which belongs to this pump, of this model, which is covered by this manual section. That turns retrieval into a filtered lookup.
  • Graph paths are themselves evidence that the explanation can cite.

Alternatives considered

RAG onlySimpler, but it risks citing the right-sounding passage for the wrong asset.
Graph onlyPrecise, but manuals and case narratives are free text that a graph cannot hold well.

Cost: the graph has to be built and maintained. A graph path also shows a relationship in reviewed data, not the cause of today's deviation. No graph store or framework has been chosen yet.

6

Freeze an evidence pack, then check the model's claims against it

The model sees a fixed input, and its output is validated before anyone sees it.

›
Hospital: the resident's note must reference lab results and chart entries that actually exist. The pharmacist rejects any order whose dose or value does not match the chart.

Why

  • Reproducible: the same pack can be replayed later to see exactly what the model was shown.
  • Citable: every item has an ID, and every hypothesis must point to IDs.
  • Checkable: a mechanical checker validates typed entity, metric, unit, grouping, time and fact-version bindings. Finding the same number somewhere in the pack is insufficient. These checks reject unsupported bindings; they do not prove a causal hypothesis correct.

Alternatives considered

Let the model search freely as it writesMore flexible, but nothing would be reproducible and citations could point to things nobody can recheck.
Trust the model's output and rely on the expert to catch errorsIt puts all the burden on a busy person and lets fluent mistakes through.

Cost: the checker sometimes rejects an output that was actually fine. The expert still has the evidence pack, so nothing is lost but the draft.

7

Fine-tune two small LoRA adapters on one open base model

An extraction adapter and a reasoning adapter. They teach skills, never facts.

›
Hospital: one doctor trained in two specialist skills: medical coding, and writing a cited assessment note. Training makes her better at how to do the job. She still reads each patient's chart instead of memorising it.

Why fine-tune at all

  • An untuned model is inconsistent at exactly the things IGNIS needs: strict JSON output, exact text offsets for spans, maintenance vocabulary, citing evidence IDs, and saying "I can't tell" instead of guessing.
  • Prompting gets part of the way. Fine-tuning on reviewed examples is expected to make the behaviour more consistent. That is a hypothesis, and the release gate tests it against the base model (decision 8).

Why LoRA adapters

  • An adapter is a small add-on to a frozen base model. It is cheap to train, small to store, and quick to swap or roll back.
  • One base model in memory can serve both adapters.

Why two adapters instead of one

  • The two tasks have different inputs, outputs, training data and success metrics.
  • They can be evaluated, released and rolled back independently. A bad reasoning release does not take extraction down with it.

Why a 7–8B open model, and why AMD hardware

  • Open weights can be fine-tuned and hosted locally, which matters for factory data. A 7–8B model is large enough for these narrow tasks and small enough to train with LoRA on a single GPU.
  • The proposed hardware is an AMD Instinct MI300X with ROCm. Its 192 GB of memory comfortably fits a 7–8B base model plus LoRA training. This depends on hackathon GPU access being confirmed, and no model has been selected.

Alternatives considered

No fine-tuning, prompting onlyValid and simpler. It is the baseline every adapter must beat. An adapter that does not beat it is simply not released, and the previously approved version, or manual review, stays in place.
Full fine-tuning of all weightsMuch more compute, larger artefacts, slower rollback, and more risk of degrading general ability, for no clear gain on narrow tasks.
Fine-tuning plant facts into the modelRejected outright. Facts change daily, a trained-in fact cannot be cited, and it goes stale silently. RAG and the graph supply facts at run time.
A large hosted API modelStrong out of the box, but it cannot be fine-tuned or versioned under the project's control, and plant data would leave the site.

Cost: a curated dataset, an evaluation harness and a model registry have to exist. These are real work, and they are why fine-tuning is its own loop.

8

Train offline, evaluate against the baseline, release only after approval

Never train per hourly alert or on case publication. Keep rollback targets.

›
Hospital: residents don't learn by changing their practice after every patient without supervision. They train on reviewed cases, pass an exam, and a credentialing committee grants privileges, which it can also withdraw.

Why

  • Unverified outcomes are not truth. Training on every new case would teach the model from cases nobody has confirmed yet.
  • No evaluation, no evidence. Without a held-out comparison there is no way to know whether the change helped or hurt.
  • Drift and poisoning: continuous training lets mistakes compound, and a few bad records could shift the model.
  • Accountability: a named model owner approves each version, and the registry records base model, adapter, dataset version, metrics and the rollback target.

Alternatives considered

Online or continual learningAlways current, but unevaluated and unaccountable. Rejected.
Automatic release when training loss improvesLower loss does not mean better citations or fewer unsupported claims. Rejected: training loss alone never qualifies a release.

Cost: improvements reach the ward weeks later rather than immediately. Facts still reach it immediately, because a published case is retrievable at once through RAG.

9

The model is useful, but the system works without it

If the adapter is unavailable, the alert and evidence still reach the expert. No silent substitution.

›
Hospital: if the resident is off sick, the nurse still pages the attending with the vital signs and the chart. Nobody asks an unqualified stand-in to sign notes in the resident's name.

Why

  • Detection, evidence gathering and dispatch must keep working through a model outage, an evaluation failure or a rollback.
  • Quietly swapping in the untuned base model would put an unevaluated model's text in front of the expert under the fine-tuned model's authority.
  • The degraded mode is honest: no drafted explanation, and extraction falls back to manual review.

What changed

An earlier draft made the fine-tuned output a required step before the expert. I changed that, because it made the model a single point of failure for safety-relevant alerts.

Cost: during an outage the expert does more reading. That is a safer failure than a missing alert.

10

A person approves every action that touches the real world

Expert scope, dispatcher approval, technician acceptance, recovery verification, publication, report sign-off and model release.

›
Hospital: the attending authorises the procedure, the scheduler books theatre, the surgeon agrees to operate, someone confirms recovery, and the case conference decides what goes into the handbook.

Why

  • Every one of these steps has consequences for safety, money or future decisions, and needs a named, accountable person.
  • The model has no approval authority anywhere. It drafts, and people decide.
  • Gates create an audit trail: who decided what, when, and on which evidence.

Alternatives considered

Fully automatic dispatchFaster on paper, but a wrong automatic booking sends a person to the wrong machine with the wrong permit.
One single approval at the endToo late. By then the work has already been done on an unreviewed diagnosis.

Cost: each gate adds minutes. In the simplified page the walkthrough no longer pauses at gates, but the gates are still part of the design.

11

Eligibility first, then minimise production loss

Hard rules filter out who can go; cost decides among those left.

›
Hospital: an uncredentialed surgeon is never booked, however available. Among credentialed surgeons, the most urgent patient gets the first available one.

Why

  • Safety and qualification rules must never be traded off against a favourable cost score, so they are constraints, not weights.
  • The goal is total production loss across all open jobs, not "nearest person to this job". The fixture shows why: the locally obvious choice costs $150 more.
  • Running and stopped machines lose production differently. Travel time is downtime only for a stopped machine.

Alternatives considered

Nearest or first-available technicianSimple, but ignores how expensive each job's downtime is.
A full constraint solver from day oneThe right tool once shared crews, parts and calendars compete. It is overkill for a two-job demo, so a transparent exhaustive comparison comes first.

Cost: it needs real qualification, calendar and loss-rate data, none of which exists yet. The fixture numbers are fictional.

12

Verify recovery before closing, and publish knowledge only after review

A failed check reopens the incident. Publication is immediately retrievable, but never trains automatically.

›
Hospital: the patient is discharged when the vital signs recover, not when the operation ends. Lessons go into the handbook after the case conference. Wrong diagnoses are discussed too, labelled as wrong.

Why

  • Repairs fail. Closing a case on "work done" would hide that and teach the system the wrong lesson.
  • Rejected and inconclusive cases are kept and labelled. They are what teach the reasoning adapter when to abstain.
  • Separating "retrievable now" (RAG) from "trained later" (fine-tuning) gives fast fact updates without risky model updates.

Cost: cases stay open longer, and someone has to run the review. Both are deliberate.

Fine-tuning in depth

Fine-tuning was the new requirement, so this section explains it on its own: what it is for, what goes into it, and how a release earns its place.

The single most important distinction: skills versus facts

A resident is trained to read charts, reason from evidence and write clear notes. She is not trained by memorising every patient's chart. Each morning she reads the current chart. IGNIS splits the work the same way.

Fine-tuning teaches skills

  • Produce strict, schema-valid JSON every time.
  • Mark component, fault and action spans with exact character offsets.
  • Use maintenance vocabulary correctly.
  • Attach evidence IDs to every hypothesis.
  • Say "insufficient evidence" instead of guessing.

Fine-tuning never supplies facts

  • Today's readings, baselines and limits come from trusted data.
  • Which manual applies comes from the knowledge graph.
  • Relevant passages and past cases come from RAG.
  • Every number, forecast, count and schedule is computed by code.
  • Approvals and bookings are made by people.
Adapter 1 · in Ingest & review

Extraction adapter, the medical coder

Input: raw work-order text, for example "replaced worn seal on P-101, leaking at gland".
Output: JSON spans such as component seal, fault worn and leaking, action replaced, each with exact offsets.

Trained on: MaintIE train and validation splits, plus approved annotations from the review queue.

Measured by: span F1 and schema validity on held-out data. The MaintIE test split is never touched.

If unavailable: the supervisor annotates manually, as before.

Adapter 2 · in Evidence & explanation

Reasoning adapter, the resident's note

Input: a frozen evidence pack (condition facts, retrieved passages, graph paths).
Output: JSON hypotheses with evidence IDs, stated uncertainty and a suggested inspection, or an explicit abstention.

Trained on: expert-reviewed cases, including rejected and inconclusive ones labelled as such. Synthetic cases for the first demo.

Measured by: citation accuracy, unsupported-claim rate and correct abstention.

If unavailable: the expert receives the alert and the evidence pack without a draft.

When does a cycle run?

Deliberately, when enough new reviewed cases have accumulated to make a better dataset. Never on a timer tied to alerts, and never because a case was published. Publishing makes a case useful immediately through retrieval. Training only changes how well the model uses evidence, so it can wait.

The same release block also carries optional updates to numeric rules and baselines, shown as the dashed arrow to the hourly check. Those are approved separately: a change to what counts as a deviation is a different decision from a change to how explanations are written.

Why 11 parts and not 36

The detailed map in ignis-data-flow.html has 36 blocks and 47 arrows. The previous high-level page had 16 runtime components, 3 separate fine-tuning modules and about 29 arrows. You found it clunky, and you were right.

The rule I used: a block earns its place on the high-level view only if it has a different owner, a different speed or a different human gate from its neighbours. Anything else is an internal step and belongs in the details panel.

  • Validation, identity resolution and text extraction share one owner and one gate, so they became Ingest & review.
  • History, snapshots, the search index and the graph are all "trusted state", so they became Trusted data.
  • Baseline, deviation and forecast run together once an hour, so they became Hourly check.
  • Graph resolution, retrieval, evidence pack, model and claim checker are one fenced explanation step, so they became Evidence & explanation.
  • The three fine-tuning modules (dataset, training, evaluation and registry) share one owner and one approval, so they became one block with four steps.

Nothing was deleted from the design. It was folded inward. The detailed map still exists for engineers, but it predates fine-tuning and has not been updated.

Where the analogy breaks

Every analogy is wrong somewhere. These are the places where thinking "hospital" would mislead you about IGNIS.

Adapters don't learn on the job

A human resident gets a little better with every patient. An adapter does not change at all between releases. Every improvement waits for the next approved cycle.

Real monitors alarm instantly

Hospital alarms are continuous. IGNIS assesses hourly by explicit choice, so an event at 10:01 may wait until 11:00. The fast critical-alarm path is optional and off by default.

The forecast is not a prognosis

"Crosses 85 °C at about 11:15" is a straight-line extrapolation of three hourly means against a fictional limit. It is not a failure time, a remaining useful life or a probability.

The cost model is money, not harm

Dispatch minimises estimated production loss. Safety is never weighed against cost; it is a hard eligibility rule, much stricter than the analogy implies.

The "resident" can be absent without a stand-in

Hospitals always cover a shift. IGNIS deliberately runs with no explanation at all rather than substitute an unevaluated model.

The chart is not omniscient

The knowledge graph only knows what reviewed data recorded. A graph path shows a relationship, not the cause of today's problem.

Glossary

Adapter
A small set of extra trained weights attached to a frozen base model. It changes behaviour for one task and can be swapped or removed.
LoRA
Low-rank adaptation: a common, cheap way to train adapters by learning small matrices instead of updating every weight.
Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)
Training on input–output examples that show the desired behaviour.
Base model
The untuned open model the adapters attach to. It is also the baseline every adapter must beat.
RAG
Retrieval-augmented generation: fetch relevant documents at run time and give them to the model, so facts come from sources rather than memory.
Knowledge graph / "KAG"
A structured map of assets, sensors, components, manuals and cases and how they relate. In IGNIS it decides what applies before RAG retrieves.
Evidence pack
The frozen bundle of facts, passages and graph paths, each with an ID, that the reasoning adapter is allowed to use.
Claim checker
Code that rejects any number, asset or time scope in the model's output that does not appear in the evidence pack.
Abstention
The model explicitly saying the evidence is insufficient. Treated as a correct answer when it is true.
Held-out data
Examples never used in training, kept aside for honest evaluation.
MaintIE
A public dataset of annotated maintenance work-order text, used for training and evaluating extraction.
Model registry
The record of every released model version: base, adapter, dataset, metrics, approver and rollback target.
ROCm
AMD's open GPU software stack, the counterpart to NVIDIA's CUDA.
vLLM
An open-source model-serving engine that can serve several LoRA adapters on one base model.
Human gate
A point where a named person must approve before work moves on.
